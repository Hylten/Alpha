A private credit fund manager prices a loan book once a quarter. The marks reflect the state of the market as it was, not as it is. An institutional LP reviews the portfolio in May and sees valuations that lagged the market by six to ten weeks. The manager defends the methodology. The LP accepts the explanation but quietly reallocates. The pricing gap is not fatal, but it is structural, and the market is now building the infrastructure to close it.

This memo traces the infrastructure shift. Apollo's daily pricing pledge, Versana's data platform, the Financial Stability Board's systemic observation and PIMCO's skepticism together form a picture of a market moving toward continuous pricing. The implications for origination, underwriting and LP relationships are direct.

## The Daily Pricing Pledge

Per Reuters on May 6, 2026, Apollo Global Management announced it will offer daily pricing for its private credit portfolio once its assets under management surpass 1 trillion USD, approximately 10.4 trillion SEK at current exchange rates. The firm intends to provide this capability across its direct lending and asset-backed lending portfolios by the fourth quarter of 2026.

Apollo's stated rationale is transparency. The pledge moves the firm away from the quarterly mark convention that has governed private credit since its early years. A daily mark changes what the LP sees, when the LP sees it and how the LP evaluates the portfolio between reporting cycles.

The structural consequence for origination is underappreciated. A fund that prices daily knows the real-time value of every position. An origination team that knows where the mark sits at any given moment can price new opportunities against a live book rather than against a stale mark. The spread between the new deal and the existing portfolio is visible in real time. That changes underwriting discipline and capital allocation speed.

## The Skeptic's View

The pricing change has critics. Per Bloomberg, as covered by Advisor Perspectives on May 14, 2026, PIMCO's Imene Raalati-Karoui questioned whether a 6 percent return in private credit is truly risk-free relative to public markets. The skepticism is directional. PIMCO's argument frames private credit returns as compensation for illiquidity, complexity and credit risk, not for any structural advantage.

The debate is useful. PIMCO's position reminds the market that daily pricing does not reduce risk. It exposes it. A daily mark that reveals a position's real value under stress forces the fund to hold capital against that risk more explicitly than a quarterly mark would. The benefit for LPs is visibility. The cost for funds is higher capital discipline and lower capacity for multiple expansion in the book.

The secondary market dimension matters. Per the same Bloomberg analysis, private credit will develop secondary markets and eventually derivatives. Daily pricing is a prerequisite for both. A secondary market requires a known value to transact against. Derivatives require a reference price. Apollo's pledge moves the market closer to both instruments, and the LP base will eventually expect liquidity mechanisms that quarterly marks never supported.

## The Infrastructure Layer

The pricing pledge depends on data infrastructure. Per PRNewswire on April 16, 2026, Versana raised 43 million USD, approximately 447 million SEK, in a growth funding round led by BNP Paribas, with Apollo joining as a new investor alongside existing backers Deutsche Bank and Wells Fargo. The platform provides real-time loan-level data across the private credit market.

Versana's role is foundational. Real-time pricing requires real-time data. A fund that prices daily needs a platform that aggregates loan-level information, updates it continuously and makes it available to portfolio managers, risk teams and LPs on the same timeline. Versana is building that infrastructure. Apollo's investment in Versana while simultaneously pledging daily pricing is not coincidental. The data layer enables the pricing layer.

Per the same Versana announcement, the firm now covers multiple asset classes across the private credit ecosystem and serves banks, non-bank lenders, asset managers, insurers, pension funds, law firms and rating agencies. The platform's expansion from syndicated loan data into direct lending and broader private credit mirrors Apollo's own portfolio breadth. The two moves reinforce each other.

## The Systemic Observation

The Financial Stability Board weighed in on the same timeline. Per its May 2026 report on private credit, the FSB estimated the market at 1.5 to 2 trillion USD, approximately 16 to 21 trillion SEK, and noted that it now serves larger companies and a broader investor base than it did a decade ago. The FSB's core concern is that the market at this size has not been tested in a severe downturn, and its interconnections with banks, insurers and private equity firms are deepening.

The FSB's observation matters for pricing. An untested market can misprice credit in both directions. Daily pricing improves visibility but does not remove the underlying risk. A fund that prices daily knows the value of its positions more precisely. It also knows, in real time, when those positions deteriorate. The transparency cuts both ways, and the FSB's warning is that the interconnections at 1.5 to 2 trillion USD mean that a correction in one segment can propagate through the system faster than quarterly marks would have revealed.

## What This Means for Origination

For origination teams, daily pricing changes three things. First, new deal pricing can be benchmarked against the live book in real time. The spread between a new origination and the existing portfolio is visible at the moment of decision rather than weeks later. Second, LP reporting becomes a continuous process rather than a quarterly event. LPs who receive daily or near-daily portfolio data will expect a corresponding level of transparency from the fund's origination pipeline. Third, risk management becomes an active, real-time function rather than a retrospective exercise.

The commercial implication is direct. Funds that control the data layer, whether through proprietary systems or platforms like Versana, gain an origination advantage. They can price faster, allocate more precisely and demonstrate transparency to LPs in a way that quarterly marks never supported. The origination edge moves from relationships to infrastructure.

The caution is structural. Daily pricing is a visibility tool, not a risk-reduction tool. The FSB's observation that the market is untested in a downturn applies regardless of how frequently the portfolio is priced. The pricing engine improves what the market sees. It does not change what the market holds.

## Summary

Private credit is moving toward daily pricing, driven by Apollo's pledge to offer daily marks once AUM exceeds 1 trillion USD, approximately 10.4 trillion SEK, by Q4 2026. Per Reuters, the capability spans direct lending and asset-backed portfolios. Versana's 43 million USD funding round, led by BNP Paribas with Apollo as a new investor, provides the data infrastructure that makes real-time pricing operationally possible. PIMCO's skepticism, as reported by Bloomberg via Advisor Perspectives, frames the debate: daily pricing exposes risk rather than reducing it. The Financial Stability Board estimates the market at 1.5 to 2 trillion USD, approximately 16 to 21 trillion SEK, and flags that it remains untested in a downturn. For origination teams, daily pricing changes benchmarking, LP reporting and risk management in real time. The pricing engine is a transparency tool. The underlying risk requires the same discipline it always has.
